## 4. Predicting Failure Before Construction

A TVC rocket’s controllability is determined by quantities that are fixed before the vehicle ever flies. Once the motor, nozzle geometry, mass distribution, and electronics architecture are chosen, the builder has already committed to a particular combination of thrust, nozzle moment arm, pitch inertia, and loop delay. The central question is therefore not whether a controller can be tuned after construction, but whether the vehicle’s control margin can be predicted beforehand. This section maps the relationship between those measurable design quantities and control outcomes across a large simulated population, identifying which builds are naturally robust and which become difficult to stabilize before hardware is manufactured.

To evaluate this relationship, I generated 2,400 rocket designs using Latin hypercube sampling across the design space. Each design was simulated using a single-axis planar pitch model with two states: pitch angle and pitch rate. The simulation operated at 200 Hz (dt = 0.005 s) for a 3.0 s burn, with the objective of maintaining a 0° reference angle under disturbance rather than recovering from an initial attitude error. The model incorporated eleven fidelity modules: wind, backlash, slew-rate limits, latency, sensor noise, thrust variation, deadband, nonlinear aerodynamic coupling, dynamic pressure variation, realistic thrust curves, and center-of-gravity/inertia changes from propellant burn. Figure 1 provides the design-space coverage used for this analysis.

Each design was classified using four simultaneous success requirements. A vehicle was considered successful only if it remained stable (maximum pitch angle below 70°), tracked the reference accurately (RMS error below 15°), finished near the commanded orientation (terminal error below 15°), and avoided large transient excursions (peak error below 50°). These requirements produced 2,362 EASY designs, 36 FRAGILE designs, and 2 INFEASIBLE designs from the 2,400 sampled vehicles. EASY designs required robustness across gain variation, while FRAGILE designs could achieve stability but failed the stronger quality requirements.

Figure 3 shows the resulting failure map across pitch inertia and loop delay. The figure compares the 2,364 tunable designs against the 36 failed designs and reveals that failures cluster strongly toward low-inertia vehicles. However, the boundary is not a sharp cutoff: the failed designs form a gradient rather than a clean dividing line. A vehicle is not guaranteed to fail simply because it has low inertia, but low inertia increasingly removes the margin available to reject disturbances. The classification shown in Figure 3 is based on the four simultaneous success gates rather than a single error metric.
The strongest single predictor of failure was pitch inertia. A randomly selected failed design had lower pitch inertia than a randomly selected surviving design 93.7% of the time. This separation was statistically significant (Mann–Whitney p = 9.45 × 10⁻²⁰, rank-biserial correlation = 0.875, n = 36 failures and 2,364 survivors). The median failed vehicle was located at only the 5.4th percentile of the population inertia distribution, with an interquartile range of 1.1–8.9%. Additionally, 17 of the 36 failed designs were below the 5th percentile and 28 of 36 were below the 10th percentile. The median failed design had only 0.19 times the population median pitch inertia. However, this relationship should be interpreted as a gradient rather than a threshold: inertia strongly influences failure probability, but no single cutoff separates successful and unsuccessful designs. Physically, this occurs because disturbance angular acceleration scales inversely with inertia, while available corrective torque remains limited by maximum gimbal authority. Once disturbance acceleration exceeds what the actuator can counter, increasing controller gains cannot recover the lost margin.

Because inertia alone does not describe the entire system, I evaluated a multivariable model using the three primary measurable contributors: inverse pitch inertia, thrust multiplied by nozzle moment arm, and loop delay. The fit used standardized predictors, an unregularized model, and bootstrap 95% confidence intervals. All three predictors contributed positively: log(1/Iyy) had a coefficient of +2.37 [1.94, 3.14], log(T·L) had +1.77 [1.30, 2.45], and log(τ) had +2.90 [2.24, 3.92]. A combined authority term, T·L/Iyy, captured the expected relationship between actuator capability and vehicle inertia, but the raw predictor combination contained additional information. Removing the thrust-moment term reduced cross-validated AUC from 0.9829 to 0.9687, showing that thrust and moment arm still contribute to prediction even though inertia dominates the failure pattern.

An earlier interpretation suggested that inertia mattered while control authority did not, because the fitted coefficient for log(T·L) was negative. This result was traced to a modeling error: an L2-regularized regression penalized a manually added intercept column. After refitting the model without this issue, the coefficient reversed direction. This correction is why the final analysis uses an unregularized fit and treats thrust authority as a contributing factor rather than dismissing it.

A potential concern is whether the failure map could be caused by the tuning procedure itself rather than by the underlying vehicle dynamics. This would require a tuner that selects one derivative gain value and then moves the proportional gain away from that value, creating an artificial mismatch. That mechanism cannot occur in this experiment. The tuning method used for this section, autotune_grid, evaluates complete proportional–derivative gain pairs directly: 25 Kp × Kd combinations are tested, each on its own performance. There is therefore no single privileged derivative gain for a design to move away from. The later behavior associated with frozen derivative tuning requires a different algorithm and is not present in the Section 4 dataset. As additional confirmation, a full retuning changed only 1.9% of labels, but the structural argument is the primary defense: the mechanism required to create the artifact does not exist in this experiment.

Figure 4 extends this analysis by examining the performance frontier rather than the binary labels. The plot shows peak achievable success rate versus authority×delay under per-design optimal tuning and fresh evaluation seeds. The relationship is strongly negative (Spearman ρ = −0.692, p = 3.4 × 10⁻¹⁰, n = 63), indicating that designs with less favorable combinations of control authority and delay have progressively lower achievable performance. The trend is graded rather than discontinuous: there is no single boundary beyond which every vehicle becomes uncontrollable. Instead, designs become increasingly expensive to stabilize as margins shrink.

The map also highlights the limitation of binary classifications. Of the 36 positive failure cases, 29 were flagged as uncertain by the project’s own Wilson-interval check. The four-number screen achieved an AUC of 0.985 against the original labels but only 0.57 against an independent harsher probe. When controller gains were pushed to a more aggressive 2× variation, only 5 of 16 original positive cases failed. Therefore, the map is reliable as a description of the simulated labeling procedure, but the exact boundary between robust and fragile vehicles is not sharply defined. The physical trend is real; the binary line drawn on top of it is not.

The practical result is a pre-construction screening method. Before cutting material or assembling electronics, a builder can estimate four measurable quantities—thrust, nozzle moment arm, pitch inertia, and loop delay—to identify vehicles that are likely to require excessive tuning effort. Section 4 answers which builds fail and how predictable that failure is. Section 5 examines the next question: why those failures occur and which physical mechanisms create the observed transitions.
